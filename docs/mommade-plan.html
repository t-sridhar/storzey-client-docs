Section 1Purpose and approach

This plan sets out how we will grow Mom Made from a launched brand with early traction into a repeat-purchase food business over six months, beginning with a detailed 30-day plan.

Mom Made already has the hardest thing to acquire: a genuine product story that customers verify on the first bite. Natural preservation, cold-pressed oils, spices hand-ground in a mortar and pestle, and no synthetic preservatives are real differences, not claims. Our job is to make them visible, make the shop convert the attention we send it, and turn first-time buyers into households that reorder.

The approach in one line: fix the shop, then build a content engine around craft and the founder, and aim everything at the two revenue events that matter most — Diwali and Sankranti.

Why this order. Food is a low-consideration, low-ticket, high-repeat purchase. It is the opposite of a boutique sale: nobody books an appointment, and a customer decides in under a minute on a product page. That means conversion is decided by the page, not the reel, and profit is decided by whether they order a second time.

What the numbers tell us

The catalogue is the bottleneck, not the traffic. Fifty-one products carry no description and not one product has a review. A customer will not spend ₹950 on food from an unfamiliar brand with no description, no reviews and one photograph. Sending content traffic to those pages spends money to produce a bounce.

Two whole categories have never sold, and the reason is timing rather than the product. Karalu and Sweets have zero recorded orders between them across seventeen products. Sweets and savouries such as ariselu, sunnundalu, chekkalu and murukulu are overwhelmingly Sankranti and Diwali purchases. They were listed outside the festivals that sell them and never marketed into one. That is a fixable sequencing problem, not a dead category.

Attention is not the constraint either. Roughly twelve posts have produced 1,168 followers, which is a reasonable response rate. The brand does not yet have a content volume problem so much as a conversion and repeat problem.

The website is invisible to search. The site renders entirely in the browser, so search engines receive an empty page. Searches such as “avakaya online”, “Andhra chicken pickle delivery” and “gongura pickle buy online” are high-intent commercial queries that Mom Made currently cannot appear for at all. The blog route already exists and is unused.

Fifteen products carry all the demand. Spreading content across 54 items would dilute everything. The plan concentrates on a hero set.

Section 3The position we will build

Mom Made will own one idea: the pickle your mother would have made, made the way she made it.

The brand's own line — Mom Made is Home Made — is already right. What it needs is proof, repeated until it is believed.

Why this position and not “organic” or “premium”. Every food brand claims natural and premium, and customers have stopped hearing it. Mom Made can do something competitors cannot: show the mortar and pestle, show the cold-pressed oil going in, show the sun-drying, name the person making it. Method is provable and specific where adjectives are not. It also justifies the price gap against a supermarket jar without ever arguing about price.

The brand system to fix in week 1

  • A one-line promise used everywhere, derived from Mom Made is Home Made
  • The three proof points stated identically every time: hand-ground spices, cold-pressed oils, no synthetic preservatives
  • Shelf-life and storage guidance shown on every product page, since this is the first question a pickle buyer has
  • Packaging and label photography that looks as careful as the product
  • A consistent reel intro and outro, caption voice, and a Telugu and English bilingual style
  • Instagram bio, highlights and shop tabs rebuilt around categories people actually search

Section 4What we are optimising for

North-star metric: repeat purchase rate at 90 days. Secondary: first-time orders per month.

Why repeat rate rather than reach or even revenue. Pickles, podis and karam powders are consumables. A household that likes Mom Made reorders every six to twelve weeks for years, and the second order costs almost nothing to win. A food brand that acquires without retaining simply buys customers once and loses money doing it. Repeat rate is the number that decides whether this business compounds.

Section 6The content system

Content is produced in two streams. The split is deliberate and it is the operating principle of the whole plan.

Why two stations rather than one. The talking setup and the process setup need different camera positions and lighting, but both can be fixed, documented and reproduced in minutes. Station two — an overhead and a side camera over a work surface — is the single highest-retention, lowest-cost content this brand can make, because it is the product claim being demonstrated rather than asserted.

Why the mother must be station one. The brand is called Mom Made. A faceless mom is a marketing device; a real person explaining why she uses cold-pressed oil is a reason to trust the jar. It is the one asset no competitor can copy, and it costs an hour a month.

Why recipes stay in the produced 30%. Recipe content needs styling, plating and ingredients, so it is genuinely more expensive. It stays in the plan because a jar that sits unopened never gets reordered, and it also supplies the B-roll that makes the founder and process content look produced.

The monthly capture day

One day a month in the kitchen runs both stations back to back and produces the large majority of the month's content, to a written procedure.

  • Station one, founder: dressed corner of the kitchen, collection of jars visible behind; run as an interview, never a script
  • Station two, process: overhead rig plus a side camera over a work surface; shot silent with clean audio for ASMR
  • Setup cards photographed once per station: camera height and distance, lens, settings, light positions, microphone placement
  • One question, one take, one file; the operator states the question number before each take
  • Two framings at station one, wide and tight, for a free cut point
  • Three throwaway warm-up questions, never published
  • Dual-recorded audio at station one; bad sound is the main cause of reshoots
  • Target: 20 to 30 usable founder pieces plus 8 to 12 process pieces from one day

Why the process station is shot silent. Real sound — stone on spice, oil pouring, a jar sealing — is the entire appeal, and it cannot be recovered later. Clean audio captured on the day is what makes these assets reusable for a year.

Section 7Month 1 — the 30-day launch plan

Why month 1 runs catalogue repair and festive content at the same time. October and November carry Diwali, which is one of only two large revenue events in this brand's year. We cannot spend the month preparing and miss it, and we cannot drive festive traffic to pages that do not convert. So the shop gets fixed in week 0 and the first festive content publishes in week 1.

Week 0 — foundations, five working days

  • Brand and story session with the founder; lock the promise and the three proof points
  • Baseline capture: sessions, conversion rate, orders, average order value, repeat rate, follower count
  • Choose the 10 hero SKUs from recorded order data and margin
  • Write descriptions for the hero SKUs first, then the remainder
  • Review drive: contact every past buyer by WhatsApp and ask for a review and a photo
  • Build 4 bundles and 2 festive hampers with pricing
  • Brief the developer on server rendering, sitemap and Search Console
  • Instagram rebuilt: bio, highlights, category tabs, pinned posts
  • WhatsApp Business catalogue, greeting and saved replies for shelf life, shipping and bulk orders
  • Google Business Profile claimed for the Hyderabad unit
  • Shoot calendar agreed and the 30-day content plan approved in writing

Month 2 — Reach

Food creator collaborations become weekly. We build the motion pipeline so that the month 1 catalogue photography yields twenty to twenty-five extra published assets without another shoot. First paid tests run behind organically proven content, targeted to Hyderabad and then to Telugu-speaking audiences in other Indian metros.

Why widen beyond Hyderabad here and not for a boutique. Mom Made ships. Unlike a store, its addressable market is not a radius, so once the shop converts there is no reason to restrict reach to one city. Bangalore, Chennai, Mumbai, Pune and Delhi hold large Telugu populations who cannot buy this locally.

Month 3 — Search and authority

The blog goes live with two to three posts a month, written from the mother's transcripts and recipes. A recipe series starts as a recurring format. Pre-Sankranti content begins building in late December, four to six weeks ahead of the festival.

Why search matters more here than for most brands. People actively search for these products by name — avakaya, gongura pickle, sunnundalu, karam podi — with clear intent to buy. Once the site is indexable, those searches are free, compounding demand. Today Mom Made cannot appear for a single one of them.

Month 4 — Sankranti

The largest revenue month in the plan. Everything points at sweets and savouries: ariselu, sunnundalu, chekkalu, murukulu and the hamper range. Bulk and corporate gifting is offered explicitly, with an order cut-off date published and enforced.

Why this month justifies the whole engagement. Seventeen products in Sweets and Karalu have never recorded a sale, and the most likely reason is that they were never marketed into the festival that sells them. Sankranti is a Telugu household's sweet-and-savoury occasion. Content must begin in late December, because families order two to three weeks ahead and production has a lead time.

Month 5 — Retention

After the festival, attention turns to the second order. We launch refill reminders by WhatsApp timed to typical consumption, test a quarterly pantry subscription on the hero SKUs, and scale the review engine so every order requests a review automatically.

Why a subscription suits this catalogue. Pickles and podis are predictable consumables. A household that finishes a 500-gram jar in eight weeks is a forecastable reorder, and a subscription converts a marketing problem into a logistics one, which is far cheaper.

Month 6 — Expansion

Ugadi content runs, and Avakaya pre-season begins, since mango pickle is made to a narrow April to June window and Telugu households plan for it. We consolidate six months of results and take a decision on the diaspora market.

The diaspora question, stated as a decision rather than an assumption. Telugu households in the United States, United Kingdom and the Gulf pay a significant premium for authentic avakaya and cannot buy it locally. It is plausibly the highest-margin market available to Mom Made. It also requires international shipping, customs-compliant packaging, food-export documentation and a different content approach built on nostalgia. We recommend deciding in month 6 with real data, not committing now.

Why this is worth more for Mom Made than for most brands. A 54-product catalogue needs constant individual product promotion, and filming 54 items is impossible at this budget. Animating catalogue stills is the only realistic way to give every SKU its own moving asset.

9.2 Telugu and English versions

Each month we dub the two best-performing pieces from the mother's batch.

Why it matters here specifically. The core customer thinks in Telugu, whether she lives in Hyderabad, Bangalore or New Jersey. Telugu versions reach the parent generation and the diaspora; English versions reach younger urban buyers who search in English. One hour of filming serves both.

9.3 How we use AI, and how we do not

We use these tools to increase volume, never to change how food looks. For a food brand this is a commercial safeguard above all else.

  • The food is always the real food: colour, oil content, texture and portion are never enhanced or generated
  • No AI-generated people shown as customers, family or reviewers
  • No generated or invented reviews, ratings or testimonials, in any form
  • No synthetic voice imitating the founder or any identifiable person without written consent
  • AI-animated content is labelled wherever a viewer could mistake it for filmed footage
  • Every AI-assisted asset passes the same review as filmed work

Why the line is absolute on food appearance. A customer who orders from an enhanced image and opens a jar that looks different does not complain, they simply never reorder — and for a business whose economics depend on repeat purchase, that is the most expensive mistake available. Food photography may be styled and well lit. It may not be falsified.

Section 10Written content and search

From month 3, two to three posts a month on the Mom Made blog, written from the mother's transcripts and recipes. The blog route already exists in the site.

Why written content is unusually important for this brand. People search for these products by name with intent to buy, and recipe searches run all year. A reel lasts about seventy-two hours; an article ranking for “how to store avakaya” keeps arriving indefinitely. It is also the cheapest content in the plan, because the thinking happens during the mother's batch.

The prerequisite. None of this works until the site is server-rendered or prerendered and a sitemap is published. That is a developer task, it is not large, and it should be booked in week 0. Without it, articles are invisible no matter how good they are.

Recommended rhythm

  • One capture day monthly across both stations, producing roughly 70% of output, at ₹8,000 to ₹14,000
  • One recipe or festive day in the months that need it: month 1, month 4 and month 6
  • Catalogue photography once, in month 1, then re-used all year through the motion pipeline
  • Proof and packing content shot on a phone at effectively no cost

This puts ongoing production at roughly ₹18,000 to ₹40,000 a month, plus the one-off catalogue shoot in month 1. The capture day is the cheapest line in the plan and yields the most published assets, which is the whole reason the split is 70 / 30.

Why cost per published asset falls sharply from month 2. Animating catalogue stills and writing from existing transcripts adds editing hours but no crew, ingredients or styling cost. One photography day keeps producing assets for the rest of the year.

A note on affordability, stated plainly. With 36 recorded orders to date, Mom Made is an early-revenue brand. A large monthly retainer would not survive to month 6 and would fail both parties. We recommend one of three structures and a decision in week 0:

Section 14Measurement and reporting

A one-page report in the first week of every month: what we published, what worked and why, what changed, the scorecard against baseline, and next month's plan.

Attribution here is unusually clean, and we will use that. Unlike a store, every order passes through the website, so content, traffic, conversion and repeat purchase can be connected properly once Search Console and analytics are in place. Setting that up in week 0 is what makes the six-month review factual rather than anecdotal.

What we commit to. Shoot days, assets published, descriptions written, reviews collected and reports delivered are within our control and will be committed. Follower and revenue targets depend on factors we do not control, and quoting a number now would be a guess presented as a promise.

Section 16What we need from Mom Made

  • The founder's commitment to one on-camera half-day each month, calendared three months ahead. Seventy per cent of content volume depends on it.
  • Two areas of the kitchen we can dress and occupy repeatedly as fixed stations: one for talking, one overhead work surface for process.
  • Real sales data: orders, revenue, average order value and repeat rate, including WhatsApp and offline orders the website does not record.
  • Margin per SKU, so the 10 hero products are chosen on profit rather than on popularity alone.
  • A developer, or our brief accepted, for server rendering, sitemap and Search Console in week 0.
  • The past customer list with consent to contact, for the review drive.
  • Production lead times and capacity, especially for festive sweets and savouries, so campaigns do not sell what cannot be made.
  • FSSAI details, shelf-life documentation and packaging specifications for claims we make on camera.
  • A decision on whether international shipping is in scope for month 6.
  • Which languages to prioritise, and who approves Telugu scripts.

Section 17Why this plan works

It fixes conversion before it buys attention. A month of catalogue work makes every rupee spent afterwards worth more, and it costs almost nothing.

It is built on a provable difference. Hand-ground spices and cold-pressed oils can be shown on camera. Method is credible where adjectives are not.

It is cheap to sustain. Seventy per cent of output comes from one repeatable capture day at two fixed stations in the kitchen, with no models, no hair and makeup and no location hire.

It aims at the two months that matter. Diwali and Sankranti carry this catalogue, and Sankranti is exactly where the seventeen products that have never sold should sell.

It optimises for the second order. Recipe content, WhatsApp and a subscription test all target repeat purchase, which is where a food brand actually makes money.

It builds assets, not just campaigns. Catalogue photography, an indexable site, search rankings, a review base and a WhatsApp list all keep working after any single campaign stops.
